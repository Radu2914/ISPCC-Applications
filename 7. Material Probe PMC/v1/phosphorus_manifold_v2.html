Theoretical basis

Extension of the periodic representation to a three-coordinate dynamical character space

The periodic table constitutes a two-dimensional manifold: more than sixty measurable properties collapse onto period and group with recoverable physical meaning. Period and group encode position in the nuclear structure sequence. They do not encode dynamical character. Gadolinium and Lanthanum occupy the same period and adjacent groups. Their nuclear behaviour is structurally distinct.

The third coordinate is dynamical character — whether a property accumulates irreversibly (cascade), self-regulates toward equilibrium, or sits at a structural boundary. This dimension separates materials that the periodic table cannot separate, without requiring experimental data to establish it.

No information is discarded. The 12 properties are encoded into the three coordinates. The encoding is defined by physics, not by the dataset. The manifold exists in nature — the classifier reveals it.

The three coordinates

What each axis captures

Π
Cascade character
Properties that are large in scale, non-periodic, and irreversible. A material with high Π sits far from the stable centre of the periodic table — its nuclear history is one of accumulation, not return.
Atomic mass Neutron cross-section Half-life (log scale)
Nuclear role: High-Π materials are strong neutron absorbers. Their interaction with radiation is dominated by capture — irreversible, directional. Examples: Gd, B, Cd, Hf.
Ε
Equilibrium character
Properties that are bounded, periodic, and self-regulating. Displaced from equilibrium, they return. This is the character of the periodic table's repeating structure — shell-filling, electronegativity trends, thermodynamic stability.
Electronegativity Melting point Period / Group
Nuclear role: High-Ε materials are moderators and structural materials. They slow neutrons through repeated elastic collisions and maintain mechanical integrity under irradiation. Examples: H, C, Zr, Al.
Β
Boundary character
Properties at a structural transition — between metallic and non-metallic, between stable and unstable isotope configurations. The Β score peaks precisely at a separatrix: neither fully one regime nor another. The boundary is not a defect. It is the design target.
Electron affinity N/Z stability ratio
Nuclear role: High-Β materials govern controlled boundaries — criticality management, burnable poisons, neutron flux interfaces. The controlled chain reaction is itself a Β condition. Examples: B (poison), Gd (burnable), U (at criticality).

Computational specification

Encoding functions and normalisation constants

Each property is normalised by a confirmed physical constant — not by the dataset mean or range — then passed through one of three fixed basis functions selected by the property's dynamical character. Basis functions and weights are fixed across all domains and all elements. No parameters are tuned to data.

φ_Π · Cascade basis
(5/11)·sin(πx)
+ (1/11)·cos(πx)
+ (1/11)·sin(2πx)
+ (3/11)·sin(π²x)
+ (1/11)·sin(πx)·cos(π²x)

sin(π²x) carries weight 3 because π² is irrational — the function never repeats at any integer period. It is the strongest non-returning basis function available. Weights (5,1,1,3,1) encode the cascade structure, not the data.

φ_Ε · Equilibrium basis
(2/5)·exp(−e·x)

+ (2/5)·xe

+ (1/5)·exp(−e·(x−0.5)²)

All three terms use e = 2.718… as the governing constant. Near-uniform weights (2,2,1) are structurally correct for bounded properties — no single mode dominates, reflecting the self-regulating character of equilibrium.

φ_Β · Boundary basis


exp(−e·(x − 0.5)²)


A Gaussian peaked at x = 0.5. Each boundary property is normalised so that its physical separatrix maps to x = 0.5. Score is maximum precisely at the boundary condition and decays toward zero at either extreme.
